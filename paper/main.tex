\begin{abstract}
Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)-mounted reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) offer a promising solution for age-of-information (AoI)-aware data collection in Internet of Things (IoT) networks. However, practical RIS hardware exhibits phase-dependent amplitude (PDA) characteristics, and element-wise hardware variations, together with imperfect channel state information (CSI), can significantly affect communication reliability. Although deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has been increasingly investigated for RIS control, its benefits remain insufficiently established against strong model-based optimization baselines under controlled and reproducible evaluation conditions. This paper develops an audited simulation benchmark for UAV-RIS-assisted data collection to systematically investigate the effects of hardware mismatch, channel-estimation errors, optimization objectives, and measurement-based feedback on decoding performance and AoI. A decode-aware alternating optimization (AO) method is evaluated against max-min AO, calibration-assisted optimization, probing-based search, and learning-based approaches, namely soft actor-critic (SAC) and proximal policy optimization (PPO). Experiments show that the decode-aware objective improves the decoding rate by $7.7\pm3.5$ percentage points over conventional max-min AO in the default setting, without requiring additional measurements. The gain is confirmed on 15 previously unseen seeds ($7.2\pm1.3$ percentage points, all positive). Furthermore, the decode-aware method reduces average AoI by $1.65\pm0.58$ time slots (approximately 14\%) under a greedy AoI-based scheduler. The results also reveal that hardware mismatch introduces comparatively modest performance degradation at realistic variation levels, while rough hardware calibration can recover most of the associated loss. The learned SAC residual policy and PPO scheduler did not outperform these baselines, which underscores the need for strong baselines and held-out validation when assessing learning-based RIS control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{imely} data collection is a fundamental requirement in emerging Internet of Things (IoT) applications, including smart-city monitoring, industrial automation, environmental surveillance, and intelligent transportation systems. In these applications, the effectiveness of a communication network depends not only on the amount of information successfully transmitted but also on the freshness of the collected data. The Age of Information (AoI) \cite{kaul2012real} has therefore emerged as an important performance metric for evaluating information freshness in wireless communication networks. Unlike conventional throughput-based metrics, AoI measures the elapsed time since the generation of the most recently received information update. Consequently, maintaining a low AoI is particularly important in applications where delayed information can negatively affect monitoring accuracy, decision-making, and system responsiveness. However, achieving reliable and timely data collection in distributed IoT networks remains challenging because of limited communication resources, dynamic wireless channels, and unreliable transmission links.

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have attracted considerable research attention as flexible aerial platforms for supporting wireless communication and data collection in distributed IoT networks. Their mobility allows them to approach geographically separated devices, establish favorable communication conditions, and provide connectivity in areas where conventional terrestrial infrastructure is unavailable or insufficient. In particular, UAV-assisted communication can improve network accessibility in urban environments where buildings and other physical obstacles frequently obstruct direct communication links between IoT devices and ground-based base stations (BSs). Nevertheless, UAV-assisted communication systems still experience propagation losses, shadowing, and signal blockage, which can limit their communication performance. Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) offer an additional degree of freedom for addressing these challenges by intelligently controlling the phase shifts of electromagnetic signals to modify wireless propagation environments. Integrating RIS technology into UAV-assisted communication systems provides opportunities for establishing more reliable communication links between distributed IoT devices and ground-based BSs. Specifically, a UAV-mounted RIS can act as a mobile passive communication relay, reflecting uplink signals toward a BS when direct device-to-BS communication is severely obstructed \cite{wu2019intelligent}.

The performance of RIS-assisted communication systems depends significantly on the accuracy of the hardware models used during system design and optimization. Many conventional RIS optimization frameworks assume that each reflecting element maintains a constant unit reflection amplitude regardless of its applied phase shift. Although this idealized assumption simplifies mathematical modeling and facilitates beamforming optimization, it does not accurately represent the characteristics of practical RIS hardware. In actual implementations, the reflection amplitude and phase shift are intrinsically coupled, meaning that changing the phase configuration of an RIS element also affects its reflection amplitude. This behavior is commonly represented through a phase-dependent amplitude (PDA) model \cite{abeywickrama2020intelligent}, which describes the nonlinear relationship between the reflection amplitude and the applied phase shift. Consequently, RIS configurations optimized under ideal unit-amplitude assumptions may not achieve their expected performance when implemented using practical reflecting elements. Ignoring the PDA response can therefore lead to inaccurate channel characterization, suboptimal phase configurations, and reduced communication reliability.

In addition to the intrinsic PDA response, manufacturing variations can introduce differences in the electrical characteristics of individual RIS elements. Such hardware mismatch means that different reflecting elements may exhibit different amplitude responses even when configured with identical phase shifts. In practical deployments, the system designer may only have access to a nominal PDA model rather than the exact characteristics of every reflecting element. This discrepancy introduces uncertainty between the hardware model used during optimization and the actual reflection behavior during communication. Furthermore, imperfect channel state information (CSI) can introduce additional performance degradation because the RIS configuration is determined using estimated rather than exact cascaded channel information. These factors raise important questions concerning the relative contributions of hardware mismatch, channel-estimation errors, and optimization objectives to the overall performance of RIS-assisted communication systems. Understanding these contributions is essential for identifying whether performance improvements should primarily focus on hardware characterization, channel estimation, or the design of more appropriate optimization strategies.

Alternating optimization (AO) has been widely adopted for addressing the joint optimization of active and passive beamforming in RIS-assisted wireless communication systems. By decomposing a complicated optimization problem into smaller subproblems, AO iteratively updates the beamforming vectors at the BS and the phase configurations of the RIS. In multiuser communication scenarios, max-min optimization is particularly attractive because it attempts to maximize the minimum achievable user rate, thereby promoting fairness among simultaneously served devices. However, maximizing the minimum achievable rate does not necessarily maximize the number of devices that successfully decode their transmitted information. This distinction becomes particularly important in threshold-based data collection systems, where a device is considered successfully served only when its achievable data rate exceeds a predefined decoding threshold. Therefore, a solution that improves the minimum user rate may not necessarily provide the best decoding performance. This limitation motivates the investigation of alternative optimization objectives that directly account for successful decoding requirements rather than relying exclusively on conventional max-min rate optimization.

Another important research direction involves the application of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to RIS-assisted wireless communication systems. DRL has been investigated for addressing complex decision-making problems involving RIS phase configuration, beamforming, resource allocation, and UAV trajectory optimization. Unlike conventional model-based optimization, model-free learning approaches can potentially discover adaptive control policies through interactions with the communication environment without explicitly solving the underlying optimization problem at every decision step. Soft actor-critic (SAC) and proximal policy optimization (PPO) are representative DRL algorithms that have been investigated for continuous and hybrid decision-making problems, respectively. Their application to UAV-RIS systems offers the possibility of learning adaptive control strategies under changing channel conditions and hardware impairments. In particular, hierarchical learning architectures can combine UAV trajectory control with low-level RIS phase optimization, potentially providing an integrated solution for aerial communication and data collection.

Despite the growing interest in DRL-based RIS optimization \cite{saglam2022drl}, the actual contribution of learning-based approaches remains difficult to establish when comparisons are conducted against relatively weak model-based baselines. For example, evaluating a learning-based approach against random phase configurations or idealized RIS designs that ignore practical hardware characteristics may overestimate its actual benefits. Similarly, reporting training performance using a single random seed or a limited number of network configurations provides insufficient evidence of the generalization capability of the learned policy. Another important consideration concerns the information available to competing optimization approaches. A learning agent may receive feedback from the actual communication environment during training, whereas a conventional model-based solver may operate exclusively using estimated CSI. Consequently, fair comparisons require consistent evaluation conditions, clearly defined information assumptions, and identical performance metrics across competing methods. Without these controls, it becomes difficult to determine whether the reported improvements originate from the learning algorithm itself, a more appropriate optimization objective, additional measurements, or differences in the evaluation methodology.

Measurement-based optimization provides another potential approach for improving RIS configurations under hardware uncertainty. Instead of relying entirely on a nominal hardware model, probing-based methods evaluate the actual communication performance of candidate configurations and use the resulting feedback to guide subsequent optimization. For example, the cross-entropy method (CEM) can iteratively refine candidate RIS configurations by evaluating their measured communication rates and selecting promising solutions for subsequent iterations. Such approaches can provide valuable information about the actual hardware and propagation environment. However, their effectiveness depends strongly on the available measurement budget. A limited number of measurements may be insufficient to identify improved configurations, whereas extensive probing increases measurement requirements and operational complexity. Furthermore, the advantages of measurement-based optimization may vary under different transmit-power levels, decoding thresholds, and channel-estimation conditions. Therefore, it is important to establish when additional measurements provide meaningful performance improvements and whether carefully designed model-based optimization objectives can achieve comparable results without requiring additional measurements.

Beyond slot-level RIS optimization, the effectiveness of UAV-assisted data collection must also be evaluated at the network level. Improving decoding performance during individual communication slots does not automatically guarantee lower network-wide AoI because the UAV must also determine which device cluster to serve and how to move between different locations. The resulting scheduling problem involves both communication reliability and the temporal evolution of information freshness. Conventional heuristic scheduling strategies, including round-robin and greedy AoI-based approaches, provide practical alternatives for selecting the next device cluster. In particular, greedy AoI scheduling prioritizes clusters with relatively high accumulated information age, while travel-aware scheduling additionally considers the movement required to reach the selected cluster. Although learning-based scheduling approaches may offer adaptive decision-making capabilities, their performance should be evaluated against carefully designed heuristic alternatives. This is particularly important in environments where the scheduling problem has a relatively simple structure and conventional decision-making strategies may already provide competitive performance.

Motivated by these considerations, this paper develops an audited simulation benchmark for investigating UAV-mounted RIS-assisted IoT data collection under practical hardware mismatch and imperfect channel information. Rather than proposing another DRL algorithm, the primary objective is to systematically investigate the factors that determine decoding performance and information freshness while establishing whether learning-based components provide measurable advantages over strong model-based alternatives. The benchmark incorporates geometric line-of-sight (LoS) channel modeling, Rician fading, element-wise PDA hardware variations, cascaded channel-estimation errors, and seed-dependent network layouts and hardware instances. All competing methods are evaluated using a consistent communication-rate function, identical channel realizations, and the same underlying evaluation conditions. Paired performance differences and confidence intervals are employed to provide a more controlled and statistically meaningful assessment of the observed improvements.

The investigation is organized around three principal research questions. First, how much of the decoding-rate degradation originates from hardware mismatch and channel-estimation errors, and how much can be attributed to the optimization objective itself? Second, under what operating conditions does measurement-based probing provide advantages over model-based optimization, and how does the measurement budget influence this comparison? Third, can learning-based RIS control and UAV scheduling outperform carefully designed non-learning alternatives when evaluated under consistent information assumptions and held-out network configurations? These questions provide the foundation for examining not only the achievable performance of individual methods but also the reliability, reproducibility, and practical significance of their reported improvements.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A simulation benchmark evaluates decoding performance and AoI under PDA characteristics, element-wise hardware mismatch, and imperfect cascaded CSI, using seed-dependent layouts and hardware instances, paired comparisons, and 95\% confidence intervals.
    \item The effects of hardware mismatch, channel-estimation errors, and the optimization objective are examined separately. A decode-aware AO that needs no extra measurements is compared with max-min AO, calibration-assisted optimization, perfect-CSI diagnostics, and probing, and it yields larger gains than improving hardware-model accuracy alone. Its sensitivity to calibration accuracy, phase resolution, and channel-estimation error, and the probing budget needed to match it, are quantified.
    \item Complete data collection episodes with heuristic schedulers quantify the network-level AoI effect, and a SAC residual controller and a hybrid-action PPO scheduler are tested against model-based and heuristic baselines.
\end{itemize}

We consider a UAV-assisted Internet of Things (IoT) network where a rotary-wing UAV equipped with a Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (RIS) serves $K_{\text{total}}$ single-antenna IoT devices. The devices are uniformly distributed within a geographical area of size $D \times D$. The devices are partitioned into $C$ clusters, where each cluster $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$ contains $K$ devices. The UAV acts as a mobile relay to collect data from the IoT devices and forward it to a stationary Base Station (BS) equipped with $M$ antennas. The RIS mounted on the UAV consists of $L$ passive reflecting elements. Fig.~\ref{fig:system_model}(a) illustrates the default scenario of Section~\ref{sec:setup}: $C=10$ clusters of $K=4$ devices in a $100\,\text{m}\times100\,\text{m}$ area, a UAV at 15\,m carrying $L=64$ elements, and a BS with $M=4$ antennas at 15\,m. Because the direct device-to-BS link is blocked, each uplink signal reaches the BS only through the cascaded device-to-RIS link $\mathbf{H}_1$ and RIS-to-BS link $\mathbf{H}_2$, and a device counts as decoded when its rate $R_k$ reaches the threshold $R_{\min}$, which resets its age $A_k$. 

The 3D coordinates of the BS are fixed at $\mathbf{p}_{\text{BS}} = (x_{\text{BS}}, y_{\text{BS}}, H_{\text{BS}})$. The UAV flies at a fixed altitude $H_{\text{UAV}}$ with a time-varying horizontal trajectory $\mathbf{p}_{\text{UAV}}(t) = (x_{\text{UAV}}(t), y_{\text{UAV}}(t), H_{\text{UAV}})$. The velocity of the UAV is constrained by $\|\mathbf{v}(t)\| \leq V_{\text{max}}$, where $V_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum flying speed. The propulsion energy consumption of the rotary-wing UAV flying at velocity $V = \|\mathbf{v}(t)\|$ is modeled based on aerodynamic principles:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    P(V) &= P_0 \left(1 + \frac{3V^2}{U_{\text{tip}}^2}\right) \\
    &\quad + P_i \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{V^4}{4v_0^4}} - \frac{V^2}{2v_0^2} \right)^{1/2} + \frac{1}{2} d_0 \rho s A V^3,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $P_0$ and $P_i$ are the blade profile and induced powers, respectively. The total energy consumed over a trajectory is constrained by the UAV's battery budget $E_{\text{budget}}$.

In all episodes of our evaluation the energy budget is not binding (8--10\% of $E_{\text{budget}}$ is used), so propulsion energy does not drive any result; we therefore make no energy claims.

\subsection{Channel Model and PDA Hardware Impairment}
Unlike ideal RIS models that assume a constant unit amplitude reflection coefficient regardless of the phase shift, we adopt a practical Phase-Dependent Amplitude (PDA) model. The reflection matrix of the RIS is defined as $\mathbf{\Phi} = \text{diag}(\beta_1 e^{j\theta_1}, \dots, \beta_L e^{j\theta_L})$, where $\theta_l \in [-\pi, \pi]$ and $\beta_l \in (0, 1]$ are the phase shift and amplitude of the $l$-th element, respectively. Based on the equivalent circuit model, the amplitude $\beta_l$ is intrinsically coupled with the phase shift $\theta_l$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \beta(\theta_l) = (1 - \beta_{\text{min}}) \left( \frac{\sin(\theta_l - \bar{\theta}) + 1}{2} \right)^{\bar{\kappa}} + \beta_{\text{min}},
    \label{eq:pda}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\text{min}}$ is the minimum amplitude, $\bar{\theta}$ is the phase offset, and $\bar{\kappa}$ controls the steepness of the amplitude degradation.

We consider a cascaded communication link from the IoT devices to the BS via the UAV-mounted RIS. Let $\mathbf{g}_k \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times 1}$ denote the cascaded channel from device $k$ to the BS. The channel is modeled with Rician fading to capture both Line-of-Sight (LoS) and Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS) components:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{H} = \sqrt{\frac{K_{\text{rice}}}{1 + K_{\text{rice}}}} \mathbf{H}_{\text{LoS}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{1 + K_{\text{rice}}}} \mathbf{H}_{\text{NLoS}},
\end{equation}
where $K_{\text{rice}}$ is the Rician factor, and the path loss follows the standard free-space path loss model $PL(d) = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) + 20\log_{10}(4\pi/c)$.

The effective channel from the $k$-th device to the BS is given by $\mathbf{h}_{\text{eff},k} = \mathbf{H}_2^H \mathbf{\Phi} \mathbf{H}_1 \mathbf{w}_k$, where $\mathbf{H}_1$ is the channel from the devices to the RIS, $\mathbf{H}_2$ is the channel from the RIS to the BS, and $\mathbf{w}_k$ is the beamforming vector at the BS. The Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) for device $k$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SINR}_k = \frac{|\mathbf{h}_{\text{eff},k}^H \mathbf{w}_k|^2 P_{\text{tx}}}{\sum_{j \neq k} |\mathbf{h}_{\text{eff},k}^H \mathbf{w}_j|^2 P_{\text{tx}} + \sigma^2 \|\mathbf{w}_k\|^2},
\end{equation}
and the achievable data rate is $R_k = \log_2(1 + \text{SINR}_k)$.

\paragraph*{Hardware mismatch.} Element $l$ has its own true PDA parameters $(\beta_{\text{min},l}, \bar{\kappa}_l, \bar{\theta}_l)$, while the designer knows only the nominal $(\beta_{\text{min}}, \bar{\kappa}, \bar{\theta})$. We generate a hardware instance of mismatch level $s$ as $\beta_{\text{min},l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\beta_{\text{min}},(0.15s)^2)$ clipped to $[0.05,0.95]$, $\bar{\kappa}_l=\bar{\kappa}\exp(\mathcal{N}(0,(0.3s)^2))$, and $\bar{\theta}_l\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\theta},(0.5s)^2)$; $s=0$ means no mismatch. Fig.~\ref{fig:system_model}(b) shows one such instance for $s=0.5$, $1$ and $2$: at $s=0.5$ the true curves stay close to the nominal one, whereas at $s=2$ their minimum amplitude, steepness and peak position scatter widely, so a solver that trusts the nominal curve picks phases that are wrong for many elements. This is a plausible synthetic model, not a measured one (see Section~\ref{sec:limits}).


\paragraph*{CSI.} Solvers see the cascaded channels $\mathbf{D}_k=\text{diag}(\mathbf{h}_{2,k})\mathbf{H}_1$ plus complex Gaussian estimation error of variance $10^{-14}$ per entry; rates are always computed from the true channels and true hardware. The device--RIS link is Rician with factor $K_{\text{rice}}$ and uses geometric LoS steering; the RIS--BS link uses a separate factor $K_{\text{rice,BS}}$.

\subsection{Age of Information (AoI) Model}
We utilize Age of Information (AoI) to quantify the freshness of data collected from the IoT devices. Let $A_k(t)$ denote the AoI of device $k$ at time slot $t$. If device $k$ is scheduled and successfully transmits its data (i.e., $R_k \geq R_{\text{min}}$), its AoI is reset to 1. Otherwise, the AoI increases linearly:
\begin{equation}
    A_k(t+1) = \begin{cases} 1, & k \in \mathcal{C}_{\text{served}}(t),\; R_k(t) \geq R_{\text{min}} \\ A_k(t) + 1, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\paragraph*{Slot level (P1).} For the scheduled cluster at a given UAV position, choose the BS combiner $\mathbf{G}$ and RIS phases $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ from the CSI estimate to maximize the number of decoded devices, $\max \sum_{k}\mathbb{1}[R_k\ge R_{\text{min}}]$, with rates evaluated on the true hardware and channel.

\paragraph*{Episode level (P2).} Jointly choose the UAV velocity and the cluster to serve at each step to minimize the network-average AoI,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \min \; & \frac{1}{T K_{\text{total}}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{total}}} A_k(t) \\
    \text{s.t.}\; & \|\mathbf{v}(t)\|\le V_{\text{max}}, \quad \sum_t P(\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|)\Delta t \le E_{\text{budget}},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where each slot's decoding outcome comes from the P1 solver. Problem P1 is the part that depends on hardware and CSI; P2 adds scheduling and travel.

\section{Methods Compared}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Schedulers (episode level)}
\emph{Round robin}; \emph{greedy AoI} (serve the cluster with the largest summed AoI, flying toward it); \emph{greedy AoI with travel} (the same, discounting clusters by the travel time needed to reach them); \emph{random}; and a learned hybrid-action \emph{PPO} scheduler (categorical cluster choice plus Gaussian velocity) trained on a pool of layouts and evaluated on held-out layouts.

\subsection{RIS solvers (slot level)}
\paragraph*{AO with a max-min objective.} Alternate between (a) the zero-forcing combiner for the effective channel $\sum_l v_l \mathbf{D}_{\cdot,l}$ and (b) a per-element search over 64 phase grid points that maximizes the minimum user rate. The solver assumes an amplitude model: \emph{ideal} (unit modulus), \emph{nominal} (the designer's PDA curve), or \emph{true} (the actual hardware, which requires a calibration campaign). Evaluation always uses the true hardware.

\paragraph*{Calibration-error AO.} AO with the true PDA parameters perturbed by a fixed error of $e$ times the mismatch scale of $s=1$ ($e\in\{0.25,0.5\}$). This is a sensitivity model that answers how accurate a calibration must be; it is not a probe-based estimator.

\paragraph*{Decode-aware AO.} Same alternation, but each element is chosen to maximize $\sum_k \sigma\!\big((R_k-R_{\text{min}}(1+\epsilon))/\tau\big)+0.01\,\min_k R_k/R_{\text{min}}$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function, $\tau=0.2R_{\text{min}}$ (our initial value, used in all studies except Section~\ref{sec:robust}, which shows that a wider sigmoid is better) and margin $\epsilon=0.15$, warm-started from the max-min solution. It uses no measurements beyond the CSI estimate. (A first version with a hard decoded-count objective had flat regions that made the element choice arbitrary and lost about 20 points; we replaced it before the results reported here.)

\paragraph*{Probing baselines.} A cross-entropy method initialized at the nominal AO solution that may measure the true rate $B\in\{6,15,45\}$ times per slot (three iterations, elite fraction 0.25), and random search with 45 measurements. These receive the feedback a learning agent trains on, but at test time rather than in advance.

\paragraph*{Perfect-CSI diagnostic.} The same AO with error-free CSI, to separate the effect of channel estimation.

\paragraph*{Learned residual (SAC).} $\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{AO,nominal}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(\mathbf{x})$ with a tanh-bounded residual from a soft actor-critic policy trained on a contextual-bandit reward. We evaluated two training recipes (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
Unless stated otherwise: a $100\,\text{m}\times100\,\text{m}$ area, $C=10$ clusters of $K=4$ devices, $M=4$ BS antennas, $L=64$ RIS elements, UAV altitude 15\,m, $V_{\text{max}}=10$\,m/s, 200-step episodes, nominal PDA $\beta_{\text{min}}=0.2$ and $\bar{\kappa}=1.5$, device transmit power 10\,dBm, $R_{\text{min}}=0.5$\,bit/s/Hz, $K_{\text{rice}}=10$, $K_{\text{rice,BS}}=1$. Relative to the public code we (i) replaced oracle best-of-$N$ action selection inside the SAC wrapper by explicit probing baselines, (ii) made layouts and hardware instances seed-dependent, (iii) added geometric LoS and a separate BS-link factor, and (iv) scored all methods with one rate function. We chose $R_{\text{min}}$ and $K_{\text{rice,BS}}$ so that decoding is neither trivial nor impossible, and test their influence in Section~\ref{sec:sweeps}.

Each of the 5 seeds has its own city layout and its own hardware instance. The curve studies of Figs.~\ref{fig:calibration}--\ref{fig:probing} draw their own slots and RNG streams, so the same method can differ from Table~\ref{tab:headroom} by a sampling error of about two points (for example nominal decode-aware AO at $s=1$: $+7.3$ there, $+6.8$ in the table), within the intervals. Slot-level studies use 200 held-out slots per seed (UAV at 0, 5, 10 and 15\,m horizontal distance from the cluster center), with all methods scored on the same slot, channel and CSI estimate. We report decode rate (\% of devices with $R_k\ge R_{\text{min}}$), and the paired difference to nominal-PDA max-min AO per seed, with 95\% $t$-intervals over the 5 seeds. With five seeds the intervals are wide; paired differences are much tighter than the raw ones because they remove layout variance.

Read from top to bottom, Fig.~\ref{fig:headroom} gives the following picture. \emph{Assuming an ideal RIS is costly:} AO that ignores the PDA (top row, far left of zero) loses 14--24 points to nominal-PDA AO at every mismatch level, so modeling the hardware, even nominally, is the first-order effect. \emph{Hardware mismatch is second order at realistic spread:} knowing the true curve gains $0.2\pm1.7$ points at $s=0$, $3.2\pm2.1$ at $s=1$ and $8.7\pm2.7$ at $s=2$. \emph{A rough calibration suffices:} with a calibration error of 0.25 the decode-aware solver recovers $13.7\pm3.1$ of the $15.4\pm3.2$ points of the true-curve decode-aware solver at $s=2$. \emph{Channel-estimation error costs about 6 points:} perfect CSI gains $6.5\pm6.2$ ($s=0$) and $6.0\pm7.4$ ($s=1$) with nominal max-min AO. \emph{The objective matters as much:} decode-aware AO with the nominal model gains $7.2\pm2.3$, $6.8\pm1.5$ and $7.9\pm1.3$ points at $s=0,1,2$ with no extra measurements, i.e.\ as much as removing the estimation error. \emph{Probing gains more but costs measurements:} CEM with 15 probes gains $7.3\pm2.3$ to $11.3\pm2.6$ points and with 45 probes $13.1\pm2.8$ to $18.7\pm2.2$; with only 6 probes it is worse than AO, because a few noisy samples replace a good solution. Random search with 45 probes is 8--17 points worse than AO. Taken together, the figure separates three effects: ignoring the PDA (top row), what better hardware or CSI knowledge adds (blue and purple rows), and what a better objective or extra measurements add (green and orange rows). The green markers lie to the right of zero in every panel without any probe.

At $s=1$ the decode-aware solver keeps its gain up to $e=0.5$ ($+8.0\pm2.1$ points, against $+8.7\pm1.5$ with perfect knowledge), falls to $+4.6\pm2.1$ at $e=1$ and becomes harmful at $e=2$ ($-4.8\pm3.1$). \subsection{Learned components: negative results}
\label{sec:negative}
\paragraph*{Residual SAC.} A first recipe with an unpaired reward made the policy worse than its own physics prior (47\% against 75\% decode in the mismatch study, including at $s=0$). A second recipe uses a paired reward (reward of the residual action minus that of the prior on the same channel and CSI), zero-residual anchor samples, an $\ell_2$ penalty and a fixed entropy weight, and keeps the snapshot with the best validation gain, with the zero-residual policy as fallback. After 8000 training slots at $s=1$ the second recipe never beat the prior on validation: the final policies are $0.044$ (first recipe) and $0.018$ (second recipe) reward units below it, and the best validation snapshot has gain $0.000$, so the learned policy reduces to the AO solution (training curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:residual}). Given the headroom analysis, the room left for a policy that sees only the CSI estimate is small: the remaining gap to 45-probe search is mostly noise in the individual slot's estimate.

Fig.~\ref{fig:residual} plots the reward gain of the learned policy over its own AO prior on fresh slots during training. The first recipe rises above the prior briefly, at 2000 and 4000 slots, and then degrades; the second recipe stays below the prior throughout, and its best-validation fallback never moves from zero. Each curve is a single training run, so the figure supports the claim that these recipes did not help in this environment, not that no learned residual could.

\paragraph*{PPO scheduler.} The hybrid-action PPO scheduler trained on a pool of layouts reached an average AoI of $27.6\pm25.1$ on held-out layouts against $11.6\pm3.4$ for greedy AoI with travel (same low level, 5 seeds), so we make no claim for learned scheduling in this environment. We did not tune it exhaustively, and the result applies to this heuristic-friendly setting, in which a one-cluster-per-step AoI problem is close to solved by greedy rules.

\subsection{Statistical power, hardware realism and objective sensitivity}
\label{sec:robust}
The preceding studies use five seeds, and the decode-aware objective was designed on the same seeds. The studies below address both points. Each was specified, with its pass criterion, in a hypotheses file committed to the project repository before the study was run (see the code release); where a criterion failed we say so.

\paragraph*{Twenty seeds, with a confirmatory split.} Seeds 0--4 were used while the method was designed and are labeled exploratory; seeds 5--19 were never used for any choice and are confirmatory. On the 15 confirmatory seeds the decode-aware solver (nominal model, width $0.2R_{\min}$) gains $+7.2\pm1.3$ points over nominal max-min AO at $s=1$ and $+8.4\pm0.7$ at $s=2$; every one of the 15 seeds improves in both cases (exact one-sided sign test $p=3.1\times10^{-5}$). Over all 20 seeds the gains are $+7.2\pm1.0$ and $+8.8\pm0.7$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:power}). The five-seed estimate was therefore not inflated by the design seeds. In Fig.~\ref{fig:power}, each marker is one seed (hollow: design seeds, filled: held-out seeds); the upper panels show that the held-out markers scatter around the same level as the design markers, and the lower panels show the 95\% interval narrowing as seeds are added without drifting.

\paragraph*{Phase resolution and estimation error.} Both studies are at $s=1$ with five seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}). When the RIS phase is restricted to $2^b$ levels, the decode-aware solver, measured against the unrestricted max-min AO, gains $+5.7\pm2.1$ points at 3 bits, $+6.6\pm1.3$ at 4 bits and $+7.3\pm1.6$ at 6 bits (the unrestricted grid); at 2 bits its gain is not distinguishable from zero ($+0.7\pm2.4$) and at 1 bit it is harmful ($-12.4\pm2.9$). Max-min AO loses $5.2\pm1.1$ points at 2 bits and $21.5\pm5.2$ at 1 bit. Our criterion that the gain stays positive at every resolution therefore fails at 1 bit, and the benefit applies to RIS hardware with at least 3-bit phase control.

For channel-estimation error we vary the variance of the CSI estimation noise by four orders of magnitude, which covers an error power from about $-25$ to $+15$\,dB relative to the mean cascaded-channel power. Decode-aware AO gains $+10.1\pm2.9$ points at the lowest error level and $+8.3\pm2.7$ at the level used elsewhere in this paper, but only $+4.8\pm4.4$ and $+0.2\pm2.6$ at the two highest levels, where the estimate is nearly uninformative. In that regime probing is far better ($+35.2\pm2.6$ and $+40.7\pm2.6$ points for 46 measurements), and so is a max-min solver with perfect CSI ($+44.1\pm13.7$ and $+59.4\pm9.5$). Decode-aware AO is a good-CSI method: it should not be used when the estimate is worse than the channel itself. In Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}, panel (a) reads left to right from coarse to fine phase control and panel (b) from accurate to useless CSI; the decode-aware curve (green) stays above max-min AO in (a) from 2 bits, and in (b) it is overtaken by probing and by perfect-CSI max-min AO as the error grows.

\paragraph*{Sensitivity to the sigmoid width and margin.} Fig.~\ref{fig:tau}(a) varies the width $\tau/R_{\min}$ and the margin $\epsilon$ of the decode-aware objective on the five design seeds. The gain over nominal AO is positive, with an interval excluding zero, in all 20 settings (from $+7.8$ to $+15.7$ points), so the benefit is not an artifact of a hand-set value. It is also not flat: the width $0.2R_{\min}$ that we used for all other experiments is close to the worst of the grid ($+7.9$ at margin 0.15), and wider sigmoids are better ($+15.2$ at $0.8R_{\min}$); the margin has little effect.

A grid optimum found on the same seeds that produced it is not evidence, so we repeated the choice honestly. We extended the widths to $\{0.2,0.4,0.8,1.6,3.2,6.4\}R_{\min}$, chose $\tau^\star$ on the exploratory seeds at $s=1$ only ($\tau^\star=1.6R_{\min}$), and evaluated it on the 15 confirmatory seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:tau}(b), (c)). It gains $+15.2\pm1.5$ points at $s=1$ and $+17.6\pm1.2$ at $s=2$, which was not used for the choice, with all 15 seeds improving in both cases, and it beats the width $0.2R_{\min}$ by $+7.5\pm0.8$ and $+9.5\pm1.0$ points in a paired comparison. The gain is flat for widths above $1.6R_{\min}$ ($+15.2\pm1.6$ at 3.2 and $+14.9\pm1.5$ at 6.4), so the result does not rest on a finely tuned value. All other experiments in this paper use the original width $0.2R_{\min}$; their decode-aware numbers are therefore conservative (see Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Results come from simulation with a synthetic hardware-mismatch model and a Rician/LoS channel; no ray-traced or measured channels are used. All parameter sweeps are one-at-a-time, and the number of seeds is five in most studies (20 in the headline and width studies), so intervals there are wide. The headline decode-aware numbers in the headroom, calibration, sweep and episode studies use the initial sigmoid width $0.2R_{\min}$, which Section~\ref{sec:robust} shows is far from the best choice, so those gains are conservative; only the probing-budget and regime comparisons were repeated with the tuned width. Decode-aware AO fails with very coarse (1-bit) phase control and when the CSI error power approaches the channel power. Pilot overhead and RIS switching time are not modeled. The episode study uses two heuristic schedulers and no learned scheduler. The residual policy was trained for one hardware instance per seed and on one seed in the final recipe; a stronger or differently structured learner could behave differently.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented an audited simulation benchmark for UAV-mounted RIS-assisted IoT data collection under practical PDA characteristics, hardware mismatch, and imperfect cascaded CSI. The results demonstrate that the optimization objective is a major determinant of decoding performance. The proposed decode-aware AO method outperformed conventional max-min AO in every tested setting without requiring additional measurements, achieving a decoding-rate improvement of $7.2\pm1.3$ percentage points on held-out seeds at the default mismatch level. With a wider sigmoid selected using exploratory seeds, the improvement increased to $15.2\pm1.5$ percentage points. At the network level, decode-aware AO also reduced average AoI by approximately 14\% under the greedy scheduler with travel.

The findings further indicate that hardware mismatch at the modeled spread has a comparatively modest impact, whereas inaccurate CSI and coarse RIS phase resolution can substantially limit optimization performance. Measurement-based probing adds the most at high transmit power, although a zero-measurement decode-aware AO with the tuned width already matches the gain of about 58 probes per slot. In our setting, the evaluated SAC and PPO methods did not outperform their model-based and heuristic baselines. These results highlight the importance of decoding-oriented objectives and rigorous baseline comparisons when evaluating learning-based UAV-RIS optimization.

Future work will investigate measured hardware and channel models, pilot and RIS switching overhead, learned components trained with more data and multiple seeds, and more comprehensive joint optimization of RIS configuration and UAV scheduling to assess practical performance under realistic deployment conditions.
