\begin{abstract}
Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) mounted on UAVs can keep the information of IoT devices fresh, but practical unit cells have a phase-dependent amplitude (PDA) response that a designer knows only approximately. Recent work addresses this with deep reinforcement learning (DRL), usually without strong model-based baselines, several seeds, or held-out layouts. We build an audited UAV-RIS data-collection benchmark and ask what limits the successful decode rate and what learning adds. Under a controlled protocol (per-seed layouts and hardware instances, paired differences, 95\% confidence intervals), we find that (i) the objective of the classical alternating optimisation (AO) matters more than hardware knowledge: a decode-aware objective raises decode rate by $7.7\pm3.5$ percentage points over max-min AO with zero extra measurements and improves in all 12 parameter settings we tested (the default and 11 one-at-a-time variations), and the gain is confirmed on 15 seeds not used in the design ($+7.2\pm1.3$ points, all 15 seeds improve); a wider sigmoid chosen on the design seeds only reaches $+15.2\pm1.5$ points on those held-out seeds, and the benefit disappears under 1-bit phase control or when the CSI error power approaches the channel power; (ii) hardware mismatch costs only a few points at realistic spread, and a rough calibration (error of 25\% of the mismatch scale) recovers most of it; (iii) probing the true rate $B$ times per slot gains more, but its advantage depends on the regime: a zero-probe decode-aware AO at the tuned width equals the gain of about 58 probes per slot, beats 46 probes at low transmit power, and loses to them at high power; (iv) at the episode level, the decode-aware solver lowers average AoI by $1.65\pm0.58$ time slots ($\approx$14\%) under the best heuristic scheduler. A physics-guided residual SAC policy and a hybrid-action PPO scheduler, which we implemented and trained, did not beat these baselines; we report them as negative results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{imely} data collection is central to smart-city monitoring and industrial IoT, and the Age of Information (AoI) \cite{kaul2012real} measures how fresh the collected data is. In urban settings, blockage cuts the direct links between IoT devices and the base station (BS). UAVs carrying a reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) can restore these links by reflecting uplink signals towards the BS \cite{wu2019intelligent}, and a moving UAV can serve distributed device clusters over time.

Most RIS designs assume that each element reflects with unit amplitude whatever phase it applies. Real unit cells do not: the reflection amplitude depends on the phase shift, and a phase-dependent amplitude (PDA) model describes this coupling \cite{abeywickrama2020intelligent}. A designer works with a nominal PDA curve, while the fabricated surface differs from it element by element. Deep reinforcement learning has been proposed for RIS control under such hardware impairments \cite{saglam2022drl}, and hierarchical variants combine a trajectory agent with a phase-control agent. These studies typically compare against weak baselines (random phases or an ideal-RIS design that ignores the hardware), report training curves from a single seed and layout, and let the learner use information that a deployed controller would not have. It is therefore unclear what the learning component contributes.

This paper does not propose a new DRL algorithm. We instead build a benchmark in which every method is scored by the same rate function on the same channel realisation and CSI estimate, and ask three questions. (Q1) Where does the loss in decode rate come from: hardware mismatch, channel-estimation error, or the optimisation objective? (Q2) How much does measuring the true rate per slot (probing) add, and when? (Q3) Do learned components (a residual policy for the RIS and a PPO scheduler) beat well-designed non-learning baselines? Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{An audited benchmark.} A UAV-RIS data-collection simulator with geometric LoS channels, per-element hardware mismatch, cascaded CSI with estimation error, and per-seed layouts and hardware instances. All results report paired differences with 95\% confidence intervals.
    \item \textbf{A decomposition of the decode-rate loss.} Max-min AO loses to a decode-aware AO that needs no extra measurements; hardware mismatch and channel-estimation error account for smaller parts (Section~\ref{sec:headroom}).
    \item \textbf{A calibration-sensitivity and probing study.} A rough calibration recovers most of the mismatch loss, and the benefit of per-slot probing is regime-dependent (Sections~\ref{sec:headroom}--\ref{sec:sweeps}).
    \item \textbf{Episode-level evidence and negative results.} The decode-aware solver lowers AoI inside full episodes with heuristic scheduling, whereas a physics-guided residual SAC policy and a PPO scheduler did not beat the baselines (Sections~\ref{sec:episode}--\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{itemize}
Our simulator and the SAC low-level wrapper descend from the public code accompanying Saglam \emph{et al.}~\cite{saglam2022drl}; we state what we changed in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Network Architecture}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/system_model.jpeg}
    \caption{System model of the proposed UAV-mounted RIS-assisted IoT network. A rotary-wing UAV acts as a mobile data collection relay, flying above $C$ IoT clusters to minimize the network-wide Age of Information (AoI, $A_k$). The uplink transmission from the $k$-th IoT device to the Base Station (BS) is established via a cascaded channel ($\mathbf{H}_1$ and $\mathbf{H}_2$), as the direct link is assumed to be severely blocked. The inset details the practical Phase-Dependent Amplitude (PDA) hardware impairment of the RIS, highlighting the fundamental non-linear coupling $\beta(\theta)$ between the reflection amplitude and the phase shift, which fundamentally constrains the achievable beamforming gain.}
    \label{fig:system_model}
\end{figure*}

We consider a UAV-assisted Internet of Things (IoT) network where a rotary-wing UAV equipped with a Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (RIS) serves $K_{\text{total}}$ single-antenna IoT devices. The devices are uniformly distributed within a geographical area of size $D \times D$. The devices are partitioned into $C$ clusters, where each cluster $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$ contains $K$ devices. The UAV acts as a mobile relay to collect data from the IoT devices and forward it to a stationary Base Station (BS) equipped with $M$ antennas. The RIS mounted on the UAV consists of $L$ passive reflecting elements. 

The 3D coordinates of the BS are fixed at $\mathbf{p}_{\text{BS}} = (x_{\text{BS}}, y_{\text{BS}}, H_{\text{BS}})$. The UAV flies at a fixed altitude $H_{\text{UAV}}$ with a time-varying horizontal trajectory $\mathbf{p}_{\text{UAV}}(t) = (x_{\text{UAV}}(t), y_{\text{UAV}}(t), H_{\text{UAV}})$. The velocity of the UAV is constrained by $\|\mathbf{v}(t)\| \leq V_{\text{max}}$, where $V_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum flying speed. The propulsion energy consumption of the rotary-wing UAV flying at velocity $V = \|\mathbf{v}(t)\|$ is modeled based on aerodynamic principles:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    P(V) &= P_0 \left(1 + \frac{3V^2}{U_{\text{tip}}^2}\right) \\
    &\quad + P_i \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{V^4}{4v_0^4}} - \frac{V^2}{2v_0^2} \right)^{1/2} + \frac{1}{2} d_0 \rho s A V^3,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $P_0$ and $P_i$ are the blade profile and induced powers, respectively. The total energy consumed over a trajectory is constrained by the UAV's battery budget $E_{\text{budget}}$.

In all episodes of our evaluation the energy budget is not binding (8--10\% of $E_{\text{budget}}$ is used), so propulsion energy does not drive any result; we therefore make no energy claims.

\subsection{Channel Model and PDA Hardware Impairment}
Unlike ideal RIS models that assume a constant unit amplitude reflection coefficient regardless of the phase shift, we adopt a practical Phase-Dependent Amplitude (PDA) model. The reflection matrix of the RIS is defined as $\mathbf{\Phi} = \text{diag}(\beta_1 e^{j\theta_1}, \dots, \beta_L e^{j\theta_L})$, where $\theta_l \in [-\pi, \pi]$ and $\beta_l \in (0, 1]$ are the phase shift and amplitude of the $l$-th element, respectively. Based on the equivalent circuit model, the amplitude $\beta_l$ is intrinsically coupled with the phase shift $\theta_l$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \beta(\theta_l) = (1 - \beta_{\text{min}}) \left( \frac{\sin(\theta_l - \bar{\theta}) + 1}{2} \right)^{\bar{\kappa}} + \beta_{\text{min}},
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\text{min}}$ is the minimum amplitude, $\bar{\theta}$ is the phase offset, and $\bar{\kappa}$ controls the steepness of the amplitude degradation.

We consider a cascaded communication link from the IoT devices to the BS via the UAV-mounted RIS. Let $\mathbf{g}_k \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times 1}$ denote the cascaded channel from device $k$ to the BS. The channel is modeled with Rician fading to capture both Line-of-Sight (LoS) and Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS) components:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{H} = \sqrt{\frac{K_{\text{rice}}}{1 + K_{\text{rice}}}} \mathbf{H}_{\text{LoS}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{1 + K_{\text{rice}}}} \mathbf{H}_{\text{NLoS}},
\end{equation}
where $K_{\text{rice}}$ is the Rician factor, and the path loss follows the standard free-space path loss model $PL(d) = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) + 20\log_{10}(4\pi/c)$.

The effective channel from the $k$-th device to the BS is given by $\mathbf{h}_{\text{eff},k} = \mathbf{H}_2^H \mathbf{\Phi} \mathbf{H}_1 \mathbf{w}_k$, where $\mathbf{H}_1$ is the channel from the devices to the RIS, $\mathbf{H}_2$ is the channel from the RIS to the BS, and $\mathbf{w}_k$ is the beamforming vector at the BS. The Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) for device $k$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SINR}_k = \frac{|\mathbf{h}_{\text{eff},k}^H \mathbf{w}_k|^2 P_{\text{tx}}}{\sum_{j \neq k} |\mathbf{h}_{\text{eff},k}^H \mathbf{w}_j|^2 P_{\text{tx}} + \sigma^2 \|\mathbf{w}_k\|^2},
\end{equation}
and the achievable data rate is $R_k = \log_2(1 + \text{SINR}_k)$.

\paragraph*{Hardware mismatch.} Element $l$ has its own true PDA parameters $(\beta_{\text{min},l}, \bar{\kappa}_l, \bar{\theta}_l)$, while the designer knows only the nominal $(\beta_{\text{min}}, \bar{\kappa}, \bar{\theta})$. We generate a hardware instance of mismatch level $s$ as $\beta_{\text{min},l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\beta_{\text{min}},(0.15s)^2)$ clipped to $[0.05,0.95]$, $\bar{\kappa}_l=\bar{\kappa}\exp(\mathcal{N}(0,(0.3s)^2))$, and $\bar{\theta}_l\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\theta},(0.5s)^2)$; $s=0$ means no mismatch. This is a plausible synthetic model, not a measured one (see Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph*{CSI.} Solvers see the cascaded channels $\mathbf{D}_k=\text{diag}(\mathbf{h}_{2,k})\mathbf{H}_1$ plus complex Gaussian estimation error of variance $10^{-14}$ per entry; rates are always computed from the true channels and true hardware. The device--RIS link is Rician with factor $K_{\text{rice}}$ and uses geometric LoS steering; the RIS--BS link uses a separate factor $K_{\text{rice,BS}}$.

\subsection{Age of Information (AoI) Model}
We utilize Age of Information (AoI) to quantify the freshness of data collected from the IoT devices. Let $A_k(t)$ denote the AoI of device $k$ at time slot $t$. If device $k$ is scheduled and successfully transmits its data (i.e., $R_k \geq R_{\text{min}}$), its AoI is reset to 1. Otherwise, the AoI increases linearly:
\begin{equation}
    A_k(t+1) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } k \in \mathcal{C}_{\text{served}}(t) \text{ and } R_k(t) \geq R_{\text{min}} \\ A_k(t) + 1, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\paragraph*{Slot level (P1).} For the scheduled cluster at a given UAV position, choose the BS combiner $\mathbf{G}$ and RIS phases $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ from the CSI estimate to maximise the number of decoded devices, $\max \sum_{k}\mathbb{1}[R_k\ge R_{\text{min}}]$, with rates evaluated on the true hardware and channel.

\paragraph*{Episode level (P2).} Jointly choose the UAV velocity and the cluster to serve at each step to minimise the network-average AoI,
\begin{equation}
    \min \; \frac{1}{T K_{\text{total}}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{total}}} A_k(t) \quad \text{s.t.}\; \|\mathbf{v}(t)\|\le V_{\text{max}},\; \sum_t P(\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|)\Delta t \le E_{\text{budget}},
\end{equation}
where each slot's decoding outcome comes from the P1 solver. Problem P1 is the part that depends on hardware and CSI; P2 adds scheduling and travel.

\section{Methods Compared}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Schedulers (episode level)}
\emph{Round robin}; \emph{greedy AoI} (serve the cluster with the largest summed AoI, flying towards it); \emph{greedy AoI with travel} (the same, discounting clusters by the travel time needed to reach them); \emph{random}; and a learned hybrid-action \emph{PPO} scheduler (categorical cluster choice plus Gaussian velocity) trained on a pool of layouts and evaluated on held-out layouts.

\subsection{RIS solvers (slot level)}
\paragraph*{AO with a max-min objective.} Alternate between (a) the zero-forcing combiner for the effective channel $\sum_l v_l \mathbf{D}_{\cdot,l}$ and (b) a per-element search over 64 phase grid points that maximises the minimum user rate. The solver assumes an amplitude model: \emph{ideal} (unit modulus), \emph{nominal} (the designer's PDA curve), or \emph{true} (the actual hardware, which requires a calibration campaign). Evaluation always uses the true hardware.

\paragraph*{Calibration-error AO.} AO with the true PDA parameters perturbed by a fixed error of $e$ times the mismatch scale of $s=1$ ($e\in\{0.25,0.5\}$). This is a sensitivity model that answers how accurate a calibration must be; it is not a probe-based estimator.

\paragraph*{Decode-aware AO.} Same alternation, but each element is chosen to maximise $\sum_k \sigma\!\big((R_k-R_{\text{min}}(1+\epsilon))/\tau\big)+0.01\,\min_k R_k/R_{\text{min}}$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function, $\tau=0.2R_{\text{min}}$ (our initial value, used in all studies except Section~\ref{sec:robust}, which shows that a wider sigmoid is better) and margin $\epsilon=0.15$, warm-started from the max-min solution. It uses no measurements beyond the CSI estimate. (A first version with a hard decoded-count objective had flat regions that made the element choice arbitrary and lost about 20 points; we replaced it before the results reported here.)

\paragraph*{Probing baselines.} A cross-entropy method initialised at the nominal AO solution that may measure the true rate $B\in\{6,15,45\}$ times per slot (three iterations, elite fraction 0.25), and random search with 45 measurements. These receive the feedback a learning agent trains on, but at test time rather than in advance.

\paragraph*{Perfect-CSI diagnostic.} The same AO with error-free CSI, to separate the effect of channel estimation.

\paragraph*{Learned residual (SAC).} $\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{AO,nominal}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(\mathbf{x})$ with a tanh-bounded residual from a soft actor-critic policy trained on a contextual-bandit reward. We evaluated two training recipes (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
Unless stated otherwise: a $100\,\text{m}\times100\,\text{m}$ area, $C=10$ clusters of $K=4$ devices, $M=4$ BS antennas, $L=64$ RIS elements, UAV altitude 15\,m, $V_{\text{max}}=10$\,m/s, 200-step episodes, nominal PDA $\beta_{\text{min}}=0.2$ and $\bar{\kappa}=1.5$, device transmit power 10\,dBm, $R_{\text{min}}=0.5$\,bit/s/Hz, $K_{\text{rice}}=10$, $K_{\text{rice,BS}}=1$. Relative to the public code we (i) replaced oracle best-of-$N$ action selection inside the SAC wrapper by explicit probing baselines, (ii) made layouts and hardware instances seed-dependent, (iii) added geometric LoS and a separate BS-link factor, and (iv) scored all methods with one rate function. We chose $R_{\text{min}}$ and $K_{\text{rice,BS}}$ so that decoding is neither trivial nor impossible, and test their influence in Section~\ref{sec:sweeps}.

Each of the 5 seeds has its own city layout and its own hardware instance. The curve studies of Figs.~\ref{fig:calibration}--\ref{fig:regime} draw their own slots and RNG streams, so the same method can differ from Table~\ref{tab:headroom} by a sampling error of about two points (for example nominal decode-aware AO at $s=1$: $+7.3$ there, $+6.8$ in the table), within the intervals. Slot-level studies use 200 held-out slots per seed (UAV at 0, 5, 10 and 15\,m horizontal distance from the cluster centre), with all methods scored on the same slot, channel and CSI estimate. We report decode rate (\% of devices with $R_k\ge R_{\text{min}}$), and the paired difference to nominal-PDA max-min AO per seed, with 95\% $t$-intervals over the 5 seeds. With five seeds the intervals are wide; paired differences are much tighter than the raw ones because they remove layout variance.

\emph{Assuming an ideal RIS is costly:} AO that ignores the PDA loses 14--24 points to nominal-PDA AO at every mismatch level, so modelling the hardware, even nominally, is the first-order effect. \emph{Hardware mismatch is second order at realistic spread:} knowing the true curve gains $0.2\pm1.7$ points at $s=0$, $3.2\pm2.1$ at $s=1$ and $8.7\pm2.7$ at $s=2$. \emph{A rough calibration suffices:} with a calibration error of 0.25 the decode-aware solver recovers $13.7\pm3.1$ of the $15.4\pm3.2$ points of the true-curve decode-aware solver at $s=2$. \emph{Channel-estimation error costs about 6 points:} perfect CSI gains $6.5\pm6.2$ ($s=0$) and $6.0\pm7.4$ ($s=1$) with nominal max-min AO. \emph{The objective matters as much:} decode-aware AO with the nominal model gains $7.2\pm2.3$, $6.8\pm1.5$ and $7.9\pm1.3$ points at $s=0,1,2$ with no extra measurements, i.e.\ as much as removing the estimation error. \emph{Probing gains more but costs measurements:} CEM with 15 probes gains $7.3\pm2.3$ to $11.3\pm2.6$ points and with 45 probes $13.1\pm2.8$ to $18.7\pm2.2$; with only 6 probes it is worse than AO, because a few noisy samples replace a good solution. Random search with 45 probes is 8--17 points worse than AO.

\paragraph*{How accurate must the calibration be?} Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration} replaces the true curve by one with calibration error $e$ (in units of the $s=1$ spread) and reports the gain over nominal max-min AO. At $s=1$ the decode-aware solver keeps its gain up to $e=0.5$ ($+8.0\pm2.1$ points, against $+8.7\pm1.5$ with perfect knowledge), falls to $+4.6\pm2.1$ at $e=1$ and becomes harmful at $e=2$ ($-4.8\pm3.1$). At $s=2$ it stays positive over the whole range ($+15.3\pm2.9$ at $e=0.5$, $+12.3\pm1.5$ at $e=1$, $+5.6\pm2.2$ at $e=2$). Max-min AO is more fragile: at $s=1$ it is no better than nominal AO at $e=0.5$ ($-0.3\pm3.0$) and loses $12.2\pm1.9$ points at $e=2$. A calibration that is accurate to about half the mismatch spread therefore suffices for the decode-aware solver.

The decode-aware solver improves on max-min AO in all 12 settings (the default and 11 one-at-a-time variations), with intervals excluding zero in each, from $+3.8\pm1.5$ points ($R_{\text{min}}=0.25$, where decoding is already easy at 90.6\%) to $+19.4\pm7.5$ (0\,dBm). The margin $\epsilon$ has little effect ($+7.5$, $+7.5$, $+8.2$ for $\epsilon=0,0.3,0.5$). The comparison with probing depends on the regime. At low transmit power, a zero-probe decode-aware AO ($+19.4$) beats 45 probes ($+11.8$); at $R_{\text{min}}=1.0$ the true-curve decode-aware solver ($+25.5$) matches 45 probes ($+22.8$). At 20\,dBm probing wins clearly (45 probes $+22.6$ against $+4.7$ for the decode-aware solver), and with $L=128$ it also leads ($+9.9$ against $+6.5$). Knowing the true curve alone (max-min AO) adds only about $+1$ to $+5$ points in every setting. We did not run a full factorial; conclusions are about one-at-a-time changes.


\paragraph*{Probing budget.} Fig.~\ref{fig:probes} varies the number $B$ of true-rate measurements per slot at $s=1$ (the starting solution counts as one measurement, so $B=1$ returns the start exactly). Decode-aware AO is shown at the tuned sigmoid width $\tau^\star=1.6R_{\min}$ of Section~\ref{sec:robust}; the studies that use the initial width $0.2R_{\min}$ are in the repository. CEM started from the max-min solution gains $+5.0\pm0.7$ points at $B=10$, $+9.6\pm1.4$ at $B=16$, $+14.8\pm2.4$ at $B=46$ and $+16.6\pm2.7$ at $B=91$, whereas the zero-probe tuned decode-aware solver gains $+15.4\pm3.0$. The two ingredients are complementary: started from the tuned decode-aware solution, CEM gains $+16.7\pm3.2$ at $B=10$ and $+21.0\pm4.2$ at $B=46$.

\subsection{Learned components: negative results}
\label{sec:negative}
\paragraph*{Residual SAC.} A first recipe with an unpaired reward made the policy worse than its own physics prior (47\% against 75\% decode in the mismatch study, including at $s=0$). A second recipe uses a paired reward (reward of the residual action minus that of the prior on the same channel and CSI), zero-residual anchor samples, an $\ell_2$ penalty and a fixed entropy weight, and keeps the snapshot with the best validation gain, with the zero-residual policy as fallback. After 8000 training slots at $s=1$ no checkpoint beat the prior (Table~\ref{tab:residual}), so the learned policy reduces to the AO solution. Given the headroom analysis, the room left for a policy that sees only the CSI estimate is small: the remaining gap to 45-probe search is mostly noise in the individual slot's estimate.

\subsection{Statistical power, hardware realism and objective sensitivity}
\label{sec:robust}
The preceding studies use five seeds, and the decode-aware objective was designed on the same seeds. The studies below address both points. Each was specified, with its pass criterion, before it was run (\texttt{paper/PREREGISTRATION.md}); where a criterion failed we say so.

\paragraph*{Twenty seeds, with a confirmatory split.} Seeds 0--4 were used while the method was designed and are labelled exploratory; seeds 5--19 were never used for any choice and are confirmatory. On the 15 confirmatory seeds the decode-aware solver (nominal model, width $0.2R_{\min}$) gains $+7.2\pm1.3$ points over nominal max-min AO at $s=1$ and $+8.4\pm0.7$ at $s=2$; every one of the 15 seeds improves in both cases (exact one-sided sign test $p=3.1\times10^{-5}$). Over all 20 seeds the gains are $+7.2\pm1.0$ and $+8.8\pm0.7$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:power}). The five-seed estimate was therefore not inflated by the design seeds.

\paragraph*{Phase resolution and estimation error.} Both studies are at $s=1$ with five seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}). When the RIS phase is restricted to $2^b$ levels, the decode-aware solver, measured against the unrestricted max-min AO, gains $+5.7\pm2.1$ points at 3 bits, $+6.6\pm1.3$ at 4 bits and $+7.3\pm1.6$ at 6 bits (the unrestricted grid); at 2 bits its gain is not distinguishable from zero ($+0.7\pm2.4$) and at 1 bit it is harmful ($-12.4\pm2.9$). Max-min AO loses $5.2\pm1.1$ points at 2 bits and $21.5\pm5.2$ at 1 bit. Our criterion that the gain stays positive at every resolution therefore fails at 1 bit, and the benefit applies to RIS hardware with at least 3-bit phase control.

For channel-estimation error we vary the variance of the CSI estimation noise by four orders of magnitude, which covers an error power from about $-25$ to $+15$\,dB relative to the mean cascaded-channel power. Decode-aware AO gains $+10.1\pm2.9$ points at the lowest error level and $+8.3\pm2.7$ at the level used elsewhere in this paper, but only $+4.8\pm4.4$ and $+0.2\pm2.6$ at the two highest levels, where the estimate is nearly uninformative. In that regime probing is far better ($+35.2\pm2.6$ and $+40.7\pm2.6$ points for 46 measurements), and so is a max-min solver with perfect CSI ($+44.1\pm13.7$ and $+59.4\pm9.5$). Decode-aware AO is a good-CSI method: it should not be used when the estimate is worse than the channel itself.

\paragraph*{Sensitivity to the sigmoid width and margin.} Fig.~\ref{fig:tau}(a) varies the width $\tau/R_{\min}$ and the margin $\epsilon$ of the decode-aware objective on the five design seeds. The gain over nominal AO is positive, with an interval excluding zero, in all 20 settings (from $+7.8$ to $+15.7$ points), so the benefit is not an artefact of a hand-set value. It is also not flat: the width $0.2R_{\min}$ that we used for all other experiments is close to the worst of the grid ($+7.9$ at margin 0.15), and wider sigmoids are better ($+15.2$ at $0.8R_{\min}$); the margin has little effect.

A grid optimum found on the same seeds that produced it is not evidence, so we repeated the choice honestly. We extended the widths to $\{0.2,0.4,0.8,1.6,3.2,6.4\}R_{\min}$, chose $\tau^\star$ on the exploratory seeds at $s=1$ only ($\tau^\star=1.6R_{\min}$), and evaluated it on the 15 confirmatory seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:tau}(b), (c)). It gains $+15.2\pm1.5$ points at $s=1$ and $+17.6\pm1.2$ at $s=2$, which was not used for the choice, with all 15 seeds improving in both cases, and it beats the width $0.2R_{\min}$ by $+7.5\pm0.8$ and $+9.5\pm1.0$ points in a paired comparison. The gain is flat for widths above $1.6R_{\min}$ ($+15.2\pm1.6$ at 3.2 and $+14.9\pm1.5$ at 6.4), so the result does not rest on a finely tuned value. All other experiments in this paper use the original width $0.2R_{\min}$; their decode-aware numbers are therefore conservative (see Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:limits}
Results come from simulation with a synthetic hardware-mismatch model and a Rician/LoS channel; no ray-traced or measured channels are used. All parameter sweeps are one-at-a-time, and the number of seeds is five in most studies (20 in the headline and width studies), so intervals there are wide. The headline decode-aware numbers in the headroom, calibration, sweep and episode studies use the initial sigmoid width $0.2R_{\min}$, which Section~\ref{sec:robust} shows is far from the best choice, so those gains are conservative; only the probing-budget and regime comparisons were repeated with the tuned width. Decode-aware AO fails with very coarse (1-bit) phase control and when the CSI error power approaches the channel power. Pilot overhead and RIS switching time are not modelled. The episode study uses two heuristic schedulers and no learned scheduler. The residual policy was trained for one hardware instance per seed and on one seed in the final recipe; a stronger or differently structured learner could behave differently.

\section{Conclusion}
On an audited UAV-RIS data-collection benchmark, the largest controllable losses in decode rate came from the optimisation objective and from ignoring the PDA hardware, not from a lack of learning. A decode-aware alternating optimisation with no extra measurements improves decode rate in every setting we tried, is confirmed on held-out seeds (and roughly doubles with a wider sigmoid chosen on the design seeds), and lowers AoI by about 14\% under a good scheduler; it needs at least 3-bit phase control and a CSI estimate better than the channel itself. Hardware mismatch is a second-order effect that a rough calibration removes, and per-slot probing adds the most at high power. A learned residual policy and a PPO scheduler did not beat these baselines. We recommend that future RIS-DRL claims be reported against such baselines, with paired statistics over seeds and held-out layouts. Code, configurations and the curated result files are released with the repository.
